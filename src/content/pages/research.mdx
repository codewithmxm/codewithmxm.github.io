## The question

In the four-storey frame I designed for my thesis, the floors hold more than half of the cradle-to-gate carbon of the building (57.6 of 102.1 kgCO₂e per m² of floor, pre-release ShellBench figures). Shells carry load through their shape rather than their thickness, so they can use much less material. In a seismic region, though, mass and stiffness decide the earthquake forces, and the design codes add minimum thicknesses, ties and diaphragm checks. I want to know when the material saving of a shell survives all of that, and when it does not.

## Three threads

**Shells under seismic loading.** [ShellBench](/work/shellbench) follows thin-shell floors through the whole design of GB 50010 / GB 50011 reinforced concrete frames, from floor to beams, columns and lateral system, and counts the A1–A3 carbon of the whole building. [ShellForm](/work/shellform) asks the same question of form-found shell roofs: does including seismic loads in form-finding save carbon over thickening a gravity-only shape? So far the answer is mostly no, because buckling and minimum thickness govern, not the earthquake.

**Durability as a carbon variable.** In pavements, the larger part of the carbon benefit of a better material can come from a longer service life rather than lower production emissions. [PaveCarbon](/work/pavecarbon) carries a laboratory durability gain through a finite-element model to an annualised carbon figure, and the [moisture-damage model](/work/moisture-model) does the same for moisture exposure over time.

**Results that can be checked.** Each project is open code with automated tests, benchmarks against closed-form or published solutions, tagged releases and, where the work is a study, a pre-registered analysis plan. My own [bachelor thesis](/work/thesis-rc-frame) is the first validation case, and its errors are logged rather than hidden.
